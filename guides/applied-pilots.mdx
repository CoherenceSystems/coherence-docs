An applied pilot is a scoped evaluation of Coherence Atlas methods in a real domain context. It is not a proof-of-concept demo or an open-ended exploration — it is a structured engagement with explicit criteria, appropriate confidentiality protections, and disciplined claim boundaries. The goal of a pilot is to evaluate whether Coherence Atlas methods are a good fit for a specific problem, using real or realistic signal environments, and to document what the evaluation actually shows. This guide covers how pilots work, how to prepare for one, and how to start a conversation about engaging.

<Note>
  Pilot configurations and data remain within the agreed scope for the duration and aftermath of the engagement. Coherence Systems does not use pilot data for product development or external reporting without explicit agreement. Confidentiality terms are established at the start of every pilot engagement.
</Note>

## Criteria for a Good Pilot Fit

Not every system or use case is a good fit for a Coherence Atlas applied pilot. Strong candidates share most of these characteristics:

<Accordion title="Multi-signal complexity">
  The system of interest generates multiple concurrent signals — sensors, metrics, logs, model outputs, or instrumentation streams — that are expected to carry relational structure across time. Single-signal or purely univariate monitoring problems are generally not well suited to Coherence Atlas methods.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Genuine drift or transition concerns">
  There is a real, documented concern about whether system behavior is gradually changing, degrading, or reorganizing — not a hypothetical interest in what drift detection might reveal. The review question should be grounded in an operational or research concern that matters to someone.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Need for interpretable evidence">
  The value being sought is not just a detection output — it is evidence that can be examined, documented, and communicated to stakeholders with explicit confidence limits. If the requirement is a simple threshold alarm, Coherence Atlas is probably not the right tool.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Appropriate signal access">
  The partner organization can provide access to a signal set representative of the domain of interest — either real operational data (with appropriate data handling arrangements) or high-quality synthetic data generated to match the real environment. Pilots cannot be meaningfully scoped without access to realistic signals.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Willingness to define evaluation criteria in advance">
  A pilot that will only define success after seeing results is not structured. Strong pilot candidates are willing to agree on evaluation criteria — what a useful finding looks like, what would count as a non-result — before analysis begins.
</Accordion>

## How to Prepare for a Pilot

Preparation work before the engagement begins determines the quality of the evaluation. Come to the initial conversation with as much of the following as you can.

<Steps>

  ### Define the Domain and Review Question

  Write the review question in one sentence. If you cannot do this yet, that is useful information — the first conversation with Coherence Systems will help sharpen it. But the more specific your starting question, the faster the pilot scoping process moves.

  Examples of well-formed review questions:
  - "Is structural coherence in our sensor network degrading between scheduled maintenance cycles?"
  - "Did the model ensemble we deployed in Q3 show drift relative to the Q2 baseline, and at what scale?"
  - "Can we detect the transition point between stable and unstable operating regimes in our simulation output?"

  ### Define the Signal Environment

  Describe the signals you intend to bring into the pilot:
  - How many signals are in scope
  - What types of signals they are (continuous sensor streams, discrete events, model outputs, etc.)
  - The time range you want to evaluate
  - Any known data quality issues, gaps, or anomalies in the record
  - Whether you are using real data, synthetic data, or a combination

  You do not need to share the actual data to begin scoping — a description is sufficient for the initial conversation.

  ### Describe the Evaluation Criteria

  What would a useful result look like for your organization? What would a non-result look like? Consider:
  - Is the goal to detect a specific known event (e.g., a maintenance cycle, a deployment, a reconfiguration)?
  - Is the goal to characterize ongoing drift between two time points?
  - Is the goal to evaluate whether a detection method is reliable enough to support an operational decision?

  Evaluation criteria do not need to be fully specified before the first conversation, but having a working draft helps both sides move quickly.

  ### Understand Confidentiality Requirements

  Come prepared to describe any data handling or confidentiality requirements that apply to your domain — regulatory, contractual, or internal policy. Coherence Systems will work with you to define terms that meet your requirements before any data is shared.

</Steps>

## The Pilot Workflow

<Steps>

  ### Scope

  Agree on the review question, signal environment, evaluation criteria, time window, and confidentiality terms. Document the agreed scope in writing before proceeding.

  A scope document should cover:
  - The review question
  - The signal set (description, not the data itself)
  - Evaluation criteria and what constitutes a finding vs. a non-result
  - Data handling and confidentiality terms
  - Timeline and checkpoints

  ### Configure

  Ingest the pilot signal set and configure Coherence Atlas for the domain. This typically includes:
  - Signal registration and context definition
  - Benchmark design and validation (for novel method configurations)
  - Baseline persistence scan to establish the reference state

  Coherence Systems staff are available throughout this stage to assist with configuration decisions.

  ### Evaluate

  Run the full analysis — persistence, drift, transition — against the pilot signal set. All analysis configurations are documented as they run so the evaluation is reproducible.

  Findings are recorded in artifact records as they develop, including the analysis configuration that produced them, the evidence base, and preliminary claim boundaries.

  ### Document

  Finalize artifact records for the pilot. Each finding includes:
  - The specific claim the evidence supports
  - The explicit limit on that claim
  - The analysis configuration and scan IDs that produced the evidence
  - Any conditions or exclusions that affected the analysis

  The pilot documentation package includes all artifact records plus a summary record linking them and recording the overall evaluation result against the agreed criteria.

  ### Review Findings

  Conduct a structured review of findings with Coherence Systems staff. This session is an opportunity to:
  - Walk through each finding and its claim boundaries
  - Discuss what the results do and do not support
  - Identify any follow-on questions the evaluation raises
  - Agree on how the documentation package will be used and by whom

  The findings review session itself is documented — its conclusions become part of the pilot record.

</Steps>

## How to Start

To begin a conversation about an applied pilot, contact Coherence Systems at [**hbengtson@coherencesystems.net**](mailto:hbengtson@coherencesystems.net) or through the [contact form](https://coherencesystems.net/contact).

In your inquiry, include:

- A one-sentence description of the review question or domain problem you are trying to address
- A brief description of the signal environment (number and type of signals, approximate time range)
- Your organization's primary goal for the evaluation (detection validation, operational review, research evaluation, other)
- Any known confidentiality or data handling requirements

You do not need a finalized scope to make first contact. The initial conversation is designed to determine whether there is a good fit and, if so, to develop the scope collaboratively.
