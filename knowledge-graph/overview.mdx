The Coherence Atlas knowledge graph is the structural backbone of the platform. Rather than treating signals as isolated streams, the graph encodes the relationships between them — how signals connect across time, how structure organizes across scales, and how system state moves between basins. This relational foundation is what allows Coherence Atlas to go beyond point-in-time observation and support genuine analysis of persistence, drift, and transition.

## Why a Graph Model

Individual signals tell you what changed. Relationships tell you what that change means.

A single anomalous reading may be noise. The same reading, connected through the graph to correlated signals at multiple scales and to a known state transition pathway, may indicate a regime shift in progress. The graph model makes those connections explicit and queryable — not inferred after the fact, but registered as part of the structural record from the moment signals are ingested.

The graph also preserves multi-scale structure natively. Fine-grained signal events, mid-scale behavioral patterns, and broad-scale system configurations all live as graph entities, with explicit relationships between them. Querying across scales is a first-class operation, not a post-processing step.

## What You Can Do with the Graph

Once your signals are registered and the graph is populated, you can:

- **Trace relationships between signals** — follow edges to understand which signals co-move, precede one another, or participate in shared structural patterns
- **Query structural evolution over time** — retrieve how the graph topology changed across a time window, surfacing where structure emerged, persisted, or degraded
- **Surface how state transitions propagate** — follow transition edges to see which signals led, which lagged, and which remained stable as the system moved between basins
- **Anchor structured reviews** — use the graph as the stable reference frame when running a Drift or Transition review, so your review question stays connected to the full signal context

## Three Primary Relationship Types

The graph encodes three categories of relationship, each corresponding to a distinct analytical dimension:

### Temporal Relationships

Temporal edges record time-ordered connections between signal events. A `PRECEDES` edge between two event nodes means one signal event was observed before another within a registered time window. Temporal edges are the basis for sequence analysis, lag detection, and causal chain tracing.

### Structural Relationships

Structural edges capture cross-scale dependencies — how fine-grained signals aggregate into mid-scale patterns, and how mid-scale patterns compose into broad-scale configurations. A `CORRELATES_WITH` edge encodes co-movement or shared structural participation without asserting a causal direction. Structural edges are what make cross-scale queries possible.

### State Relationships

State edges describe the geometry of system state. A `BELONGS_TO_BASIN` edge assigns a signal or state node to a region of the state field. A `TRANSITIONS_TO` edge records movement between basins, anchored to the time and signal context of the transition. State edges are the foundation for Transition review and regime shift analysis.
